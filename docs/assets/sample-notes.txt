Photosynthesis — Study Notes (Biology 101)

Topic 1: The light-dependent reactions

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar. It happens in
two linked stages: the light-dependent reactions, which capture light, and the
Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions), which fixes carbon dioxide.

The light-dependent reactions take place in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast.
The two photosystems involved are Photosystem II (PSII) and Photosystem I (PSI). PSII
absorbs light at roughly 680 nm and 700 nm, splits water, and releases oxygen as a
by-product. PSI absorbs light at around 700 nm and uses the energy to reduce NADP+ into
NADPH.

The electron transport chain between the two photosystems pumps protons into the
thylakoid space. Water is split at Photosystem II: 2 H2O -> 4 H+ + 4 e- + O2. The oxygen
released by this reaction is the oxygen that animals breathe. The protons accumulate
inside the thylakoid, and when they flow back through ATP synthase they drive the
synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. ATP synthase is therefore an
example of chemiosmosis.

The chain is closed by NADP+ reductase at the end of Photosystem I, which transfers the
excited electrons to NADP+ to form NADPH. So the light-dependent reactions produce
three things: ATP, NADPH and oxygen.

Topic 2: The Calvin cycle

The Calvin cycle takes place in the stroma of the chloroplast and does not need light
directly, although it depends on the ATP and NADPH made in the thylakoids. The enzyme
responsible for fixing carbon dioxide is RuBisCO (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate
carboxylase/oxygenase), which attaches CO2 to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). The
result splits into two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). Reduction uses ATP and
NADPH to convert 3-PGA into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), and about 10 of every 12
G3P molecules are used to regenerate RuBP, consuming further ATP.

RuBisCO is not efficient: it reacts with oxygen about once every few hundred turnovers,
a process called photorespiration, which wastes energy and releases CO2. C4 and CAM
plants concentrate CO2 around RuBisCO to reduce photorespiration, which is why they are
better adapted to hot, dry climates.

Topic 3: Limiting factors

The rate of photosynthesis depends on light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and
temperature. Light intensity and CO2 concentration are saturation factors: their effect
levels off once the limiting factor is no longer scarce. Temperature has an optimum,
because the enzymes involved (RuBisCO in particular) denature at high temperatures. A
classic school experiment measures the rate by counting the number of bubbles released
from an aquatic plant (Elodea) per minute, which is a proxy for oxygen production.

Review questions

1. Which photosystem splits water, and what is the by-product?
2. What are the three products of the light-dependent reactions?
3. Why does RuBisCO cause photorespiration, and how do C4 plants avoid it?
4. How does chemiosmosis connect the electron transport chain to ATP production?
